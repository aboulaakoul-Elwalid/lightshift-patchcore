Part A · Foundations

A1. The task, from zero

Visual inspection and the labelling problem

A factory camera photographs parts; a decision must be made for each: OK or defective. Supervised learning would need many labelled examples of every defect type, but defects are rare, varied, and often new (a scratch no one has seen). What a factory does have in abundance is photos of good parts. Anomaly detection turns this around: learn what "normal" looks like from good parts only, and flag whatever does not fit.

The datasets

MVTec AD

15 object and texture categories. Per category: a train set of only good images, a test set of good and defective images, and pixel masks for defects. We use bottle, hazelnut, leather. Lighting is controlled and constant: the benchmark every method nearly solves (image AUROC ≈ 1.0).

MVTec AD 2

8 harder categories (can, fabric, fruit_jelly, rice, sheet_metal, vial, wallplugs, walnuts). Defects can be tiny, poses vary, and the test sets contain real lighting changes (file names carry the tag: regular, overexposed, underexposed, shift_1…). Hidden "private" test sets are scored on MVTec's server.

The sujet (LightShift)

Asks: make PatchCore robust to lighting shift without retraining. Proposed tools: B1 augmentation, B2 statistics alignment, B3 conformal threshold, B4 a different model for comparison; evaluate on MVTec AD, validate on AD 2.

Why "lighting" specifically

A model that was calibrated in the lab will meet a different lamp, a sunny window, a dirty diffuser. If a system silently starts rejecting good parts (false alarms) or accepting bad ones (misses), the operator loses trust in it. The sujet's question is therefore practical: how few photos from the new lighting does it take to restore a trustworthy decision?

Two separate failures, and keeping them separate is the central idea. Perception failure: the scores no longer separate good from bad (AUROC falls). Decision failure: the scores still separate but the cut-off is in the wrong place (false-alarm rate explodes). B2 targets the first, B3 the second.

A2. Metrics, computed by hand

A detector produces a score s(x) for each image x (higher = more anomalous). A threshold τ turns scores into decisions: flag x if s(x) > τ.

The four outcomes

flagged (s > τ)not flagged (s ≤ τ)
defective imageTP (true positive)FN (miss)
good imageFP (false alarm)TN
TPR = recall = TP / (TP + FN) share of defects caught FPR = FP / (FP + TN) share of good parts wrongly flagged precision = TP / (TP + FP) F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)

Worked example

20 good, 10 defective. At τ: 3 good flagged, 8 defective flagged. Then FP=3, TN=17, TP=8, FN=2. FPR = 3/20 = 0.15; TPR = 8/10 = 0.80; precision = 8/11 = 0.727; F1 = 2·0.727·0.8/(0.727+0.8) = 0.762.

AUROC: threshold-free ranking quality

Sweep τ from +∞ to −∞; each τ gives a point (FPR, TPR); the curve is the ROC curve; the area under it is AUROC. It has an exact probabilistic meaning:

AUROC = P( s(defective) > s(good) ) + ½ · P( s(defective) = s(good) ) for one defective and one good image drawn at random. (Mann–Whitney U statistic / (nd · ng))

Worked example (do it on paper)

Good scores: 0.30, 0.35, 0.50. Defect scores: 0.45, 0.60, 0.80. Compare every defect with every good (9 pairs): 0.45 beats 0.30, 0.35 (not 0.50) → 2; 0.60 beats all three → 3; 0.80 beats all three → 3. AUROC = (2+3+3)/9 = 0.889.

Why AUROC alone misleads (hypothesis QR2). AUROC only looks at order. Add +0.4 to every score (a global lighting offset) and AUROC does not change at all, yet a fixed threshold now flags every good part (FPR = 100%). In our data this happens: under brightness-5 the AUROC of hazelnut stays 0.91 while the false-alarm rate at the old threshold is 100%.

Pixel-level and benchmark metrics (what AD 2 uses)

  • Anomaly map: one score per pixel/patch, showing where the anomaly is.
  • AU-PRO: for each defect region, the fraction of its pixels overlapped by the thresholded map ("per-region overlap"), averaged over regions, integrated over thresholds until the false-positive rate (on good pixels) reaches a limit. AD 2 reports AU-PRO0.05 (limit 5%). It gives small defects the same weight as large ones.
  • SegF1: F1 of the binary segmentation at a fixed threshold you supply. This is why the AD 2 submission needs a thresholded folder: the threshold choice is part of your result. It is exactly where B3 matters.

A3. Deep features: what the numbers are

PatchCore does not look at pixels. It looks at feature vectors produced by a convolutional network trained on ImageNet (1.28M natural images, 1000 classes). We never train it: its weights are frozen. It is a fixed "lens" that turns local image content into vectors where similar-looking content is close.

The network and the tensors (our exact configuration)

Deviation from the paper, stated plainly. The original PatchCore also applies a 3×3 average pool around each cell (local neighbourhood aggregation) and an adaptive pooling that projects to 1024 dims. Our own implementation (src/lightshift/patchcore.py) omits both for simplicity and speed; the reproduction gate (Part D) used anomalib's full implementation and matches the published numbers. Our experiments therefore compare variants within our pipeline, which is what an ablation needs.

Why two layers?

Early layers see fine texture, late layers see object structure. Taking layers 2 and 3 balances locality (to localise a scratch) with context (to know it is a scratch on a bottle). Layer 4 is too coarse (8×8 at this size) and too ImageNet-specific.

Why ImageNet features help when lighting is unchanged

Two patches of intact bottle glass under the same lamp produce nearly the same descriptor; a crack produces a descriptor unlike any intact patch. Distances in this space are meaningful for the lighting the bank was built under. That qualification is the root of the whole project.

Sizes you can verify

bottle: 125 bank images × 256 patches = 32,000 patch vectors; keep 25% → 8,000 × 1536 floats × 4 bytes = 49 MB, which is exactly the size of app/banks/bottle.pt. WideResNet-50-2 has about 69 M parameters, all frozen, never touched by gradient descent.

A4. PatchCore, step by step

PatchCore (Roth et al., CVPR 2022) has two phases. Fit (once, per category): fill a memory bank with patch descriptors of good images. Predict (per image): measure how far the image's patches are from the bank.

Fit

  1. For each good training image, extract its 256 descriptors (A3). L2-normalise each one, f ← f / ‖f‖, so every descriptor lies on the unit sphere.
  2. Stack them: M = all descriptors of all training images (for bottle with 125 images: 32,000 × 1536).
  3. Coreset subsampling: keep 25% of M chosen by a greedy rule (below). The result is the memory bank B (8,000 × 1536).

There is no loss function and no gradient step. "Training" is copying vectors.

Predict

for each patch descriptor qi of the test image (i = 1…256), after L2 normalisation: di = min over b ∈ B of ‖ qi − b ‖² (distance to nearest normal patch) anomaly map = (d1, …, d256) reshaped to 16×16, upsampled to the image image score s = maxi di (the worst patch decides)

The cosine identity (why the arithmetic is cheap)

For unit vectors a, b: ‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2 a·b = 2 − 2 cos θ. So the nearest neighbour is the vector with the largest dot product, and for all 256 patches against the whole bank this is a single matrix product Q Bᵀ (256 × 8000), followed by a row-wise min. This is what runs in the demo in 84 ms on a CPU.

Worked example in 2-D

Pretend descriptors are 2-D unit vectors given by an angle. Bank = angles {0°, 90°, 180°}. A test image has three patches at 10°, 80°, 135°.

patchnearest bank vectorΔθd = 2 − 2cos Δθ

The greedy coreset (k-center) and why it is safe

Keeping all 32,000 vectors is wasteful because many are near-duplicates (flat glass). We want a subset C ⊂ M such that every vector of M is close to some element of C. That is the k-center problem: minimise the covering radius r(C) = maxm∈M minc∈C ‖m − c‖.

Greedy rule: start with an arbitrary c₁. Repeat: add the point of M that is farthest from the current C. cj+1 = argmaxm ∈ M minc ∈ C ‖ m − c ‖ Guarantee (Gonzalez 1985): the covering radius of the greedy set is at most 2× that of the best possible set of the same size.

Worked example (1-D, pick 3 of 6 points)

M = {0, 1, 2, 10, 11, 12}; start C = {0}. Distances to C: (0,1,2,10,11,12) → farthest is 12, so C = {0, 12}. Distances to C: (0,1,2,2,1,0): a tie between 2 and 10 (both 2); break ties by first index → add 2. C = {0, 2, 12}. Covering radius: distances (0,1,0,2,1,0) → r = 2. The two natural clusters are both represented. A random subset such as {0, 1, 2} would leave 12 at distance 10 away: a normal patch would then be called anomalous. This is why greedy beats random.

In practice, distances in 1536 dimensions are expensive, so the greedy search runs on a random projection to 128 dimensions (Johnson–Lindenstrauss: pairwise distances are preserved up to a small distortion with high probability). Our code (greedy_coreset in patchcore.py) does exactly this. The first pilot used a cheaper strided subsample instead; we replaced it.

Why the image score is a max

A defect is local: it covers a few patches out of 256. Averaging would dilute it; the max keeps it. The price: the max is also the most sensitive statistic to any patch looking odd, including from a global lighting change. We will see that this is the exact mechanism of the failure.

What this model cannot do

  • Logical defects (wrong count, wrong position): every patch looks locally normal, so the max patch distance stays low.
  • Tiny defects at low resolution: at 256 px a pin-prick may be less than one 16×16 cell. (This is the MVTec AD 2 "can" problem, Part D.)
  • A situation absent from the bank: the bank stores the lighting it saw. New lighting = every patch is "new".

B1. Why lighting breaks a memory bank

The five lighting shifts we generate

To study robustness in a controlled way we synthesise lighting changes on the MVTec AD test and train images (module src/lightshift/shift.py, severities 1–5, fixed parameters registered before running anything). Pixel values x ∈ [0, 255]; results are clipped to [0, 255].

The mechanism of failure

Let a good image have patch distances d1…256 to the bank and score s = max di. Under a lighting shift every patch descriptor f is displaced to f′ = f + δ, where δ is a systematic offset (the network encodes brightness and colour in its channels). After L2 normalisation the displacement rotates the vector on the sphere, away from where bank vectors are. Therefore every di increases, including those of perfectly good patches, and so does the max. The bank has no entries for "this patch, under the new lamp".

Defective images also move, but less: their worst patch was already far from the bank, so there is less room to grow. In the pilot, bottle at brightness-5 goes from mean score 0.39 to 0.53 for good parts but only 0.79 to 0.83 for defective ones. The clouds slide right and the gap between them narrows. Two things can then be true at once:

  • Ranking (AUROC): as long as the gap does not close, defects still tend to outscore good parts. AUROC survives, slightly damaged.
  • Fixed threshold: the old τ now lies below the good cloud. Nearly every good part is flagged. FPR → 100%.

Precision about "AUROC survives". It is not universally true. At severity 5, shading drops bottle AUROC to 0.948, brightness drops leather to 0.947 and hazelnut to 0.913 in the pilot. The claim is: ranking degrades much less than the threshold-based decision does. The full numbers per family and severity are in Part D.

Why max-of-NN is especially fragile

A mean over patches would average the offset; a max picks the single worst patch, and under a systematic shift some patch is always among the unluckiest. The statistics literature calls this an extreme-value effect: the max of many noisy quantities is sensitive to the tail, and a shift moves the whole tail. This is the formal reason the bank cannot "learn the lighting": it only knows what it stored.

The two axes of repair

Decision axis: B3

Leave the scores alone, move the line. Estimate where the line should be from k normal photos taken under the new lighting, with a distribution-free guarantee on the false-alarm rate. Fixes FPR. Cannot fix poor ranking.

Perception axis: B2

Undo the feature offset before matching so new-lighting patches land near the bank again. Improves the scores themselves (ranking and detection power). Has no guarantee; it is a heuristic that must be tested.

Part C · The repairs

C1. B3: a conformal threshold, derived

Goal. Choose τ so that a new good part raises a false alarm with probability at most α (say 10%), without assuming anything about the shape of the score distribution. Ingredient: n good photos taken under the deployment lighting, scored by the model: S1, …, Sn.

Derivation (split conformal prediction)

Let Sn+1 be the score of a new good photo from the same lighting. Assume S1…n+1 are exchangeable (any ordering is equally likely; i.i.d. is a special case). Then the rank of Sn+1 among all n+1 values is uniform on {1, …, n+1} (if there are no ties).

Let S(k) be the k-th smallest of S1…n. Sn+1 ≤ S(k) ⇔ the rank of Sn+1 among n+1 values is ≤ k ⇒ P( Sn+1 ≤ S(k) ) = k / (n+1) We want a false alarm probability P( Sn+1 > τ ) ≤ α, so we need k/(n+1) ≥ 1 − α: k = ⌈ (n+1)(1 − α) ⌉ τ = S(k)

That is the whole proof: counting ranks. No Gaussian assumption, no model of the scores.

The statement above averages over the random calibration set. For one fixed calibration set, the true probability that a normal image scores below τ is F(τ) where F is the score CDF; and F(S(k)) is the k-th smallest of n uniforms, which is Beta(k, n+1−k). So the realised false-alarm rate (FPR) is

The "k-th largest" typo in the sujet, and your max-vs-miss objection

The sujet writes "the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th largest score". For n = 50, α = 0.1 that is k = 46: the 46th largest is the 5th smallest, a threshold near the 10th percentile, so about 90% of good parts would be flagged. The correct reading is the k-th smallest (equivalently the (n+1−k)-th largest). We fixed this in code (src/lightshift/b3.py) and tests (tests/test_b3.py).

And the point you raised during the project is exactly right and worth stating as a trade-off: a higher threshold buys fewer false alarms at the price of more misses. The most conservative choice, the maximum calibration score, minimises false alarms but lets weak defects pass. The conformal rule is the principled middle: for n = 27, k = 26, which is the second largest score, one rank below the maximum. α is the dial: lowering it moves τ up and the miss rate with it. The guarantee covers only false alarms; it says nothing about misses, which we measure empirically (TPR) in Part D.

Worked example

n = 10 calibration scores: 0.31, 0.28, 0.35, 0.30, 0.33, 0.29, 0.32, 0.34, 0.27, 0.36. Sorted: 0.27 0.28 0.29 0.30 0.31 0.32 0.33 0.34 0.35 0.36. α = 0.1 → k = ⌈11 × 0.9⌉ = ⌈9.9⌉ = 10 → τ = 0.36 (the maximum). Expected FPR = 1/11 = 9.1% ≤ 10% ✓. With n = 19: k = ⌈20×0.9⌉ = 18, τ = 2nd largest, E[FPR] = 2/20 = 10%.

What the guarantee does not cover

  • Exchangeability: calibration photos and future photos must come from the same lighting. If the lamp changes after calibration, the guarantee is void. That is why recalibration after a lamp change is a deployment step.
  • Synthetic photos are not allowed in the calibration set. If you add augmented photos, the scores are no longer exchangeable with real future scores, and the "at most α" statement silently becomes false. (Augmentation is fine for B2's statistics, which carry no guarantee.)
  • It is marginal, not conditional: on average over calibration sets. For a particular small set, the realised FPR follows the Beta law above and can be well above α.
  • It controls only the false-alarm rate on normal parts. Misses are not controlled.
  • It is at image level. For AD 2 pixel-level SegF1 we reuse the threshold as a heuristic, without a guarantee.

C2. B2: aligning feature statistics

Idea

B3 moves the line. B2 moves the scores back. Hypothesis: lighting acts on the network's feature channels roughly as a per-channel affine map, fc′ ≈ ac fc + bc. If we can estimate (a, b) from a few normal photos of the new lighting, we can invert it before the nearest-neighbour lookup, without touching the bank.

The estimates (per channel c = 1…1536)

reference moments (the lighting the bank was built under): μr,c, σr,c² = mean and variance of channel c over all patches of the bank-building images target moments (the new lighting): μt,c, σt,c² = the same, over all patches of the k calibration photos B2a (mean) f′ = f − (μt − μr) B2b (mean+std) f′ = (f − μt) / σt · σr + μr then L2-normalise f′ and look it up in the (unchanged) bank.

This is "AdaBN" (Li et al.) in spirit: re-estimate normalisation statistics on the target domain. We do it post hoc on the features rather than inside the network, and we only match first and second moments.

Worked example (two channels)

μr = (1.0, 2.0), σr = (0.5, 1.0). New lighting measured on k photos: μt = (1.5, 2.4), σt = (0.5, 2.0). A test patch f = (1.6, 2.5). B2a: f′ = f − (0.5, 0.4) = (1.1, 2.1). B2b: channel 1: (1.6−1.5)/0.5·0.5 + 1.0 = 1.1; channel 2: (2.5−2.4)/2.0·1.0 + 2.0 = 2.05. So f′ = (1.1, 2.05). Note how B2b also shrinks channel 2 because its spread doubled under the new lighting, which B2a does not do.

Why a diagonal (per-channel) model, and what it costs in samples

You asked, during the project, whether estimating a high-dimensional correction from ~10 photos is dangerous. It would be if we estimated a full covariance (CORAL-style): 1536 × 1537 / 2 ≈ 1.18 million parameters from ~2,560 correlated patches would be badly ill-conditioned. Our model is diagonal: each channel is estimated on its own. A channel mean has standard error ≈ σc/√neff; this does not grow with the number of channels. Here neff is the effective number of independent samples: between k (if all patches of one photo were identical) and 256k (if all were independent). Patches inside one photo are strongly correlated, so neff sits well below 256k, but a mean needs few samples. A variance needs more (its error also depends on the fourth moment): this predicts that B2b needs larger k than B2a. QR4 below confirms it.

The bug that made B2b look bad, and what we learned

In the first pilot, B2b "failed" in every condition. Reading the code later revealed it multiplied the centred features by σt instead of dividing by σt and multiplying by σr. Implemented correctly (src/lightshift/b2.py, with a unit test that checks the aligned features reproduce the reference moments), B2b becomes the best variant for ranking in several conditions. Lesson: a surprising negative result is a reason to read your code first; "it is a finding" was premature.

The pilot's second weakness: an oracle

The pilot estimated the mean shift as μ(shifted photos) − μ(the same photos, clean): it required the clean twin of every target photo. A factory has no clean twin of a photo taken under tonight's lamp. The deployable estimate compares the target photos against the bank's own statistics. QR4 below compares the two directly ("oracle_a" vs "b2a").

Where B2 can fail (honest list)

  • It can erase real defects. Alignment is applied to every patch, defective or not. If a defect changes per-channel statistics, and the calibration photos were not purely normal, part of the defect signal is subtracted. The sujet calls for a negative control (discoloured defects must remain detectable at ≥ 80% of reference). We did not run it. It remains open, and it is the first thing a reviewer should ask.
  • The per-channel affine model is an assumption. Spatially varying lighting (shading) violates it: one offset per channel cannot undo a gradient across the image. Surprisingly, shading is where B2 helps most in our deployable experiment (QR4): the average offset is still the dominant effect. The pilot's oracle estimate wrongly suggested the opposite; another reason to measure the deployable version.
  • B2 has no coverage guarantee; the combined pipeline's false-alarm rate is only as good as B3's recalibration on the aligned scores (which is why the app recalibrates after aligning).

Reference moments (μr, σr) = (3, 2). Target photos give (μt, σt) = (5, 4) for this channel. Compute B2a and B2b outputs for f = 9, and decide which one inverts "f′ = 2f − 1" (the true shift: a = 2, b = −1... check: does it map (3,2) to (5,4)?).
Solution
True shift f′ = 2f − 1 maps mean 3 → 5 and std 2 → 4 ✓. A patch measured at 9 under the new lighting came from f = (9+1)/2 = 5 under the reference. B2a: 9 − (5−3) = 7 (wrong: only fixes the mean). B2b: (9−5)/4·2 + 3 = 5 ✓. When the shift really is affine with a ≠ 1, B2b is the exact inverse; B2a is exact only when a = 1.

D0. How to read the evidence

Common protocol. Per category, the good training images are split with a fixed seed: 60% bank, 10% source calibration (sets the original threshold), 30% target pool (the "operator's photos": k are drawn from here). (3) With only 4–5 photos per condition, the estimate is noisy. A fair test needs a dataset with dozens of normal photos per lighting condition; AD 2's public split does not provide that. The AD 2 "can" scare, explained

Early in the project vanilla PatchCore on AD 2 can gave image AUROC ≈ 0.45 (below chance) and we feared a bug. We checked: the ImageNet weights load correctly (weights allclose to a fresh model); a minimal reimplementation outside the library gives the same result; per-lighting-condition AUROC is also ≈ 0.4; alternative score statistics (p99, top-10 mean, mean) do not help; and the median defect covers only 5.6×10−5 of a 2232×1024 image, which at 256 px is far smaller than one 16×16 feature cell. This agrees with the AD 2 paper (PatchCore AU-PRO0.05 ≈ 4.7% on can, and a large gain from higher input resolution). It is a resolution/defect-size effect, not a bug, and it is why our B2/B3 numbers on can are uninformative (nothing to repair). It would be the natural motivation for a crop-and-zoom front end (B5), which we did not build.

D8. QR2: does AUROC hide the threshold failure that F1 would show?

Registered claim. At severity ≥ 2, F1 (image level, at the source threshold) drops by ≥ 15 points more than AUROC on at least one category. Falsified if the median over categories × shifts × severities of (F1 drop − AUROC drop) is < 5 points.

F1 was computed from the stored FPR and TPR and the known test-set sizes (bottle 20 good / 63 defective, hazelnut 40 / 70, leather 32 / 92) over the 45 shifted conditions of QR4 (vanilla, source threshold).

Result. Median (F1 drop − AUROC drop) = 1.4 points (bottle 2.3, hazelnut 0.0, leather 1.4): by the registered rule the hypothesis is falsified. The ≥ 15-point effect does occur in 4 of 45 conditions, all on hazelnut (shading 2, 3, 4; brightness 4), with a maximum of 20.6 points. Verdict: falsified by its own criterion, with an instructive reason.

Why F1 hides the failure here: prevalence

The MVTec test sets are defect-heavy (63 of 83 bottle images, 70 of 110 hazelnut images, 92 of 124 leather images are defective). Take the worst case: the shifted threshold flags everything (FPR = 100%, TPR = 100%). With prevalence π of defective parts, precision = π and recall = 1, so

F1 = 2π / (1 + π) hazelnut: π = 70/110 = 0.636 → F1 = 0.778 bottle: π = 63/83 = 0.759 → F1 = 0.863

"Flag everything" still scores F1 ≈ 0.78–0.86, so F1 barely falls even though the system is useless: it rejects every good part. In a real factory defects are rare. At π = 5% the same "flag everything" behaviour gives F1 = 2·0.05/1.05 = 0.095. (This last number is arithmetic, not a measurement on our data.)

Lesson. Choose the metric to match the deployment prevalence. FPR (the false-alarm rate on good parts) does not depend on prevalence, which is why this project reports FPR at the source threshold as the decision-failure metric instead of F1. The registered claim was too tied to the benchmark's defect-heavy test sets.

Result: B3 is fragile to contamination when n is small, and B2 does not help. With k = 10 and α = 0.1 the conformal rank is k = 10, i.e. the threshold is the maximum calibration score. One defective photo among ten drags the threshold from ≈ 0.48 to ≈ 0.76 and the share of defects caught falls from 91% to 52%; with three bad photos only 29% of defects are caught. The false-alarm rate falls to 0%: the system has become blind, not careful. This is the "a high threshold swallows real defects" trade-off you raised, in its most dramatic form. B2a changes little (it is not itself damaged, but it inherits the inflated threshold).

Larger n buys robustness: the rank arithmetic

With n photos the threshold is the k-th smallest score, k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉. Defects score above normals, so as long as at least k photos are genuinely normal, the k-th smallest is still a normal score; the threshold is only inflated once the number of defective photos m exceeds n − k. For n = 10 that is 0; for n = 50 (k = 46) it is 4. So with 50 photos up to 4 mistakes (8%) are tolerated, and the cliff sits at m = 5. Measured:

Practical rule. If a human picks the calibration photos, use n ≥ 30–50 and keep the m ≤ n − k budget in mind; or screen the photos first (reject any photo whose score is far above the others, which is cheap with the score you already compute). With n = 10 the procedure is correct only if every photo is normal.
What this does not test. The registered discolouration control (a defect whose colour matches the shifted lighting, which B2 might absorb) was not run. Contamination tests the threshold and the mean estimate under bad calibration data, not whether B2 hides a single colour-matched defect in a good calibration set.

D10. What the evidence says, in one page

  1. The failure is real and mostly a decision failure. Lighting moves good scores above the original threshold: 40% false alarms on average across our synthetic shifts, up to 100%; 80–100% under real over/under-exposure in AD 2 vial. Ranking degrades much less on synthetic shifts, but on real exposure changes it degrades too (vial AUROC 0.95 → 0.72).
  2. B3 is the indispensable repair. k ≈ 10 normal photos of the new lighting bring false alarms back to about α = 10% on synthetic shifts, with the realised rate following the theory (n+1−k)/(n+1), and the same law appears on real AD 2 lighting. It requires n ≥ 9 photos for α = 0.1 and an unchanged lighting afterwards.
  3. B2 is a useful but secondary booster on synthetic shifts, and unproven on real ones. It does not change the false-alarm rate; it raises detection at the same rate where the scores are damaged (brightness, shading: TPR 87–94% → 95–100% at k = 10). B2a needs very few photos (k ≈ 5); the corrected B2b needs ≥ 25. On real AD 2 lighting with 3–4 photos it did not help.
  4. B1 (augmenting the bank) is stronger than B2 for lighting you anticipated and useless for lighting you did not. No guarantee either way.
  5. The repair is free at serving time (≈ 1× vanilla latency); the cost is the operational step "take ~10 photos after a lamp change".
  6. Feature drift predicts the damage (ρ ≈ 0.8–0.9 with the false-alarm rate), which suggests an automatic "recalibrate now" alarm.
One-sentence answer to the project's question. A memory-bank detector keeps its false-alarm promise when the lights change if you recalibrate its threshold on about ten normal photos of the new lighting (B3); realigning its features (B2) helps detection a little, only on some shifts, and was not shown to help on real lighting.

F2. What could make the conclusions wrong (threats to validity)

  • Synthetic shifts. Five simple photometric transforms are not a factory lamp. Real exposure shifts clip and move highlights (D5), where B2 did not help.
  • Three categories, one split seed. Per-category spread is large (bottle behaves differently from hazelnut). More categories and seeds would tighten every number; we report means and a few per-category breakdowns, not confidence intervals.
  • Small test sets. 20–40 good test images per category; one image = 2.5–5 points of FPR.
  • Calibration photos are held-out training photos, which may be slightly easier than test photos; this can bias the realised FPR (bottle) and is not exactly the exchangeability the proof needs.
  • Image level only. We did not evaluate B2/B3 on pixel-level metrics (AU-PRO, SegF1); the AD 2 thresholded masks reuse the image-level threshold as a heuristic.
  • Own implementation of PatchCore (no 3×3 aggregation, no 9-neighbour re-weighting). The gate used anomalib; the study compares variants inside our pipeline.
  • B2's defect-erasing risk is only partly tested (contamination check; discolouration control not run).
  • One α. All experiments use α = 0.1; the miss rate at other α is not charted.

F3. What we would do next (ordered)

  1. Run the registered discolouration negative control and a B2 test on a dataset with dozens of normals per real lighting condition.
  2. B5: class-agnostic crop + higher resolution, on AD 2 can and vial (targets the tiny-defect failure, not lighting).
  3. B4: a second model, to show whether the false-alarm collapse is PatchCore-specific.
  4. Add confidence intervals (bootstrap over test images and seeds) to QR3/QR4, and a drift-based "recalibrate now" monitor (QR1 supports it).
Part G · Study aids

G1. Pen-and-paper problem set

Attempt each on paper before opening the solution. A2.1, A4.1, C1.1 and C2.1 above are the first four.

G1.1 Memory-bank arithmetic

A category has 300 good training images at 256×256. Our features have 256 patches × 1536 dims per image, stored as float32. (a) Size of the full patch matrix? (b) Size of a 25% coreset bank? (c) Multiply-adds for scoring one image (256 patches vs the 25% bank)?

Solution
(a) 300 × 256 = 76,800 vectors × 1536 × 4 B = 471.9 MB (≈ 450 MiB). (b) 19,200 vectors × 1536 × 4 B = 118 MB. (c) 256 × 19,200 × 1536 = 7.55 × 109 multiply-adds ≈ 7.5 GMAC. With the 8,000-vector bottle bank it is 256 × 8,000 × 1536 = 3.1 GMAC, which is why one image takes milliseconds on a GPU and ≈ 84 ms on a CPU (backbone included).

G1.2 Why normalisation matters

Two descriptors a = (3, 4) and b = (6, 8) point the same way but b is twice as long. (a) ‖a − b‖²? (b) After L2 normalisation? (c) What does this say about brightness changes that rescale features?

Solution
(a) (3²+4²) = 25. (b) both become (0.6, 0.8): distance 0. (c) L2 normalisation removes any pure rescaling of a descriptor (like a global gain), but a per-channel offset or a non-uniform rescaling still rotates the vector. That is why lighting still hurts even with normalisation, and why B2 aligns before normalising.

G1.3 The spread of the realised FPR

n = 10, α = 0.1, k = 10 (the maximum). FPR ~ Beta(a, b) with a = n+1−k = 1, b = k = 10. (a) Mean? (b) Variance = ab / ((a+b)²(a+b+1)) and standard deviation? (c) Is "FPR ≈ 0.2 for this calibration set" surprising?

Solution
(a) a/(a+b) = 1/11 = 0.091. (b) var = 10 / (121 × 12) = 0.00689; sd = 0.083. (c) 0.2 is only about 1.3 standard deviations above the mean: not surprising. A single small calibration set can give an FPR twice the nominal α; the guarantee is about the average over calibration sets. In QR4 the p90 of the realised FPR at k=10 is about 0.30.

G1.4 Threshold or features?

After a lamp change, every good score rises by 0.3, every defect score by 0.1; before: good ∈ [0.2, 0.4], defect ∈ [0.6, 0.9], τ = 0.5. (a) New score ranges? (b) FPR and TPR at τ = 0.5? (c) Which repair (B3 or B2) addresses what?

Solution
(a) good ∈ [0.5, 0.7], defect ∈ [0.7, 1.0]. (b) almost all good parts exceed 0.5 → FPR ≈ 100%; TPR = 100%. (c) The ranking is intact (all defects ≥ all good only at the touching point 0.7; the gap closed from 0.2 to 0.0, which is the "gap narrows" effect). B3 moves τ to ≈ 0.7 (restores FPR; may lose a few defects at the touching point). B2 would try to lower good scores back toward [0.2, 0.4] by realigning features, which also reopens the gap. B3 cannot reopen the gap; only B2 (or a better model) can.

G4. Questions a professor may ask

Is PatchCore trained? Is it an autoencoder?
No to both. A frozen ImageNet network extracts features; "training" only stores a subset of normal patch vectors. An autoencoder learns weights by reconstruction; PatchCore uses nearest-neighbour distance to stored examples.
Why does AUROC stay high but the system fails?
AUROC measures ordering only; a systematic shift of all scores preserves much of the order but moves the fixed threshold into the good cloud. Part B, Exercise G1.4.
Prove the conformal guarantee.
Exchangeability makes the rank of the new score uniform on 1..n+1, so P(Sn+1 ≤ S(k)) = k/(n+1) ≥ 1−α when k = ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉. Section C1.
When does the guarantee fail?
Lighting changes after calibration (not exchangeable), synthetic calibration photos, n < 9 for α = 0.1, and it never controls misses.
Why did B2b fail in the pilot and work later?
The pilot multiplied by σt instead of rescaling by σr/σt. Fixed and unit-tested. Section C2.
Was the first B2 estimate realistic?
No: it used clean twins of the shifted photos (an oracle). The deployable estimate matches it closely in QR4 (so the optimism was small), but this had to be measured, not assumed.
Is the AD 2 "can" result a bug?
No: defects are pin-prick sized (median 5.6×10−5 of the image) and vanish at 256 px; the AD 2 paper reports the same for PatchCore. We verified weights, reimplemented independently, and checked per-lighting scores.
What did you not do?
See Part F: B4 (second model), B5 (object crop), the discolouration negative control, other categories, and real (non-synthetic) lighting with enough calibration photos.
Why only three MVTec AD categories?
Time and disk: the three cover a rigid object (bottle), a natural object with pose variation (hazelnut), and a texture (leather). More categories would tighten the estimates but are the same experiment.

Appendix

Appendix. Earlier lessons we kept (the clamp, and why raw scores matter)

Before this project, an earlier PatchCore study (on MVTec AD cable and MVTec LOCO splicing_connectors, July 2026) produced a write-up that shaped three decisions here. Source: smartex-lab-p0/docs/mmselec/MMSELEC_PATCHCORE_RAW_SCORE_WRITEUP_2026-11-12.html. Summary of what carried over:

1. Library scores can be clipped, and clipping destroys ranking

anomalib normalises raw distances to [0, 1] with clamp((raw − thr)/(max − min) + 0.5, 0, 1). Everything above the clamp collapses to 1.0 and everything below to 0.0, and ties count as ½ in AUROC. In the earlier study 57% of clean scores were pinned at exactly 0, and the "AUROC = 0.5000" rows seen under rotation were saturation, not a model verdict: 8 of 36 shift rows had exactly 0.5000, the fingerprint of saturation. Re-measured on raw distances the clean baseline was 0.833, not 0.789.

AUROC = P(sdefect > sgood) + ½ P(sdefect = sgood) → if every score clamps to 1.0, AUROC = ½ regardless of the model.

Consequence in this project: all our scores are raw squared distances (no clamp), and B2/B3 operate on them.

2. Ranking and calibration are different failures

In that study, severe warm colour shift made 63% of good parts fail at the source threshold (calibration failure) and also cost about 0.10 AUROC (ranking failure). A threshold fix helps the first and cannot help the second. This is the two-axis framing (B3 and B2) used throughout.

3. The conformal formula in the sujet was backwards

First spotted in that write-up: "k-th largest" must be the k-th smallest, otherwise n = 50, α = 0.1 flags about 90% of good parts. See C1.

Other things that write-up recorded (not re-verified here)

  • Logical defects (wrong count/position) are hard for PatchCore: AUROC 0.75 vs 0.84 for structural defects on splicing_connectors.
  • Rotation breaks PatchCore (raw AUROC 0.58–0.59), unlike lighting; this is the pose-variability issue behind the "crop the object first" idea (B5).
